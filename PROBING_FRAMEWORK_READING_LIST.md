# Academic Sources for a Follow-Up Probing Framework

*A reading list for designing/verifying an adaptive follow-up-question (probing) framework — e.g. for structured interviews, coaching dialogue, or tutoring systems. Organized by the specific design question each body of literature answers. All entries are real, established sources — verify exact volume/page numbers against Google Scholar/JSTOR/PsycINFO before final citation, since those details can drift in memory.*

---

## 1. Why probe at all — the learning-cycle justification

**Kolb, D.A. (1984).** *Experiential Learning: Experience as the Source of Learning and Development.* Prentice Hall.
- The foundational four-stage cycle (Concrete Experience → Reflective Observation → Abstract Conceptualisation → Active Experimentation). A probe maps to the *Reflective Observation* stage — it's what turns a raw answer into material the respondent can learn from. If your framework needs a first-principles reason "why probe instead of just scoring the first answer," this is it.

**Gibbs, G. (1988).** *Learning by Doing: A Guide to Teaching and Learning Methods.* Oxford Brookes University.
- A more granular six-stage reflective cycle (Description → Feelings → Evaluation → Analysis → Conclusion → Action Plan). Useful if you want your probe sequence to map to specific reflective sub-stages rather than one undifferentiated "reflection" step.

---

## 2. Where the follow-up should target — scaffolding and developmental gap theory

**Vygotsky, L.S. (1978).** *Mind in Society: The Development of Higher Psychological Processes.* Harvard University Press.
- The Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD): the gap between what someone can do independently and what they can do with structured support. This is the core theoretical answer to "how do you decide *where* a follow-up is needed" — target the gap, not the whole answer.

**Wood, D., Bruner, J.S., & Ross, G. (1976).** The role of tutoring in problem solving. *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry*, 17(2), 89–100.
- The original "scaffolding" paper — operationalizes Vygotsky's ZPD into an actual tutoring mechanism. If your framework needs a citable definition of *scaffolding* as a graduated-support technique (not just a metaphor), this is the source.

**Kalyuga, S. (2007).** Expertise reversal effect and its implications for learner-tailored instruction. *Educational Psychology Review*, 19(4), 509–539.
- Explains *when to stop scaffolding*: support that helps a novice actively interferes with a more capable respondent's existing schema. Critical if your framework needs to justify reducing probe intensity as competence is demonstrated, rather than applying uniform support to everyone.

**Sweller, J. (1988).** Cognitive load during problem solving: Effects on learning. *Cognitive Science*, 12(2), 257–285.
**Paas, F., Renkl, A., & Sweller, J. (2003).** Cognitive load theory and instructional design: Recent developments. *Educational Psychologist*, 38(1), 1–4.
- Cognitive Load Theory — distinguishes intrinsic, extraneous, and germane load. Useful for justifying *why* probe wording/complexity should be tightly controlled (extraneous load reduction) rather than open-ended.

---

## 3. Deciding probe difficulty/intensity over repeated interactions

**Ericsson, K.A., Krampe, R.T., & Tesch-Römer, C. (1993).** The role of deliberate practice in the acquisition of expert performance. *Psychological Review*, 100(3), 363–406.
**Ericsson, K.A., & Pool, R. (2016).** *Peak: Secrets from the New Science of Expertise.* Houghton Mifflin Harcourt.
- Deliberate Practice theory: development requires specific goals, immediate feedback, and practice at the edge of current ability — directly justifies why a probe should target a *named, specific* gap rather than ask a generic "tell me more."

---

## 4. What makes a probe/follow-up question *good* — question design and interviewing methodology

**Campion, M.A., Palmer, D.K., & Campion, J.E. (1997).** A review of structured interview research: Implications for practice. *Personnel Psychology*, 50(3), 655–702.
**Campion, M.A., Pursell, E.D., & Brown, B.K. (1988).** Structured interviewing: Raising the psychometric properties of the employment interview. *Personnel Psychology*, 41(1), 25–42.
- The core structured-interview literature — establishes why standardized, evidence-anchored follow-ups outperform free-form questioning for validity/reliability. Foundational if the framework needs to defend structured probing over ad hoc questioning.

**Levashina, J., & Campion, M.A. (2007).** Measuring faking in the employment interview: Development and validation of an interview faking behavior scale. *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 92(6), 1638–1656.
- Relevant if the framework needs to address a common probing failure mode: candidates giving rehearsed/inflated answers, and how probing can (or can't) surface that.

**Hattie, J., & Timperley, H. (2007).** The power of feedback. *Review of Educational Research*, 77(1), 81–112.
- The three-level feedback model (Task / Process / Self-regulation). Useful for sequencing follow-up depth — early probes at the task level, later ones targeting strategy/self-regulation.

**Nicol, D.J., & Macfarlane-Dick, D. (2006).** Formative assessment and self-regulated learning: A model and seven principles of good feedback practice. *Studies in Higher Education*, 31(2), 199–218.
- Practical design principles (clarify standards, facilitate self-assessment, close the gap between current and desired performance) — good source for a checklist of what a "good" follow-up should accomplish.

---

## 5. Why the respondent needs to understand *why* they're being asked

**Cable, D.M., & Kay, V.S. (2012).** Striving for self-verification during organizational entry. *Academy of Management Journal*, 55(2), 360–380.
- Self-verification theory applied to the employment context: people engage more authentically when they understand the purpose behind a question rather than perceiving it as a test. Justifies including a transparent "why we're asking this" rationale alongside the probe itself.

**Goffman, E. (1959).** *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life.* Doubleday.
**Leary, M.R., & Kowalski, R.M. (1990).** Impression management: A literature review and two-component model. *Psychological Bulletin*, 107(1), 34–47.
- Foundational impression-management theory. Include with a caution: use it to understand self-presentation dynamics, not as a "lie detector" — treating apparent impression management as a deception signal is a well-documented validity trap (no ground truth is available from a single transcript).

---

## 6. Adapting tone/framing to the individual

**Higgins, E.T. (1997).** Beyond pleasure and pain. *American Psychologist*, 52(12), 1280–1300.
- Regulatory Focus Theory (promotion vs. prevention orientation) — relevant if the framework should adjust probe *framing* (gap-closing vs. strength-building language) based on how a respondent is motivationally oriented, without changing question difficulty itself.

**Winstone, N.E., Nash, R.A., Parker, M., & Rowntree, J. (2017).** Supporting learners' agentic engagement with feedback: A systematic review and a taxonomy of recipience processes. *Educational Psychologist*, 52(1), 17–37.
- Feedback literacy / recipience framework (proactive / responsive / avoidant). Useful for justifying why probe tone (safety-framed vs. direct) should adapt to how a respondent typically engages with feedback.

**Luthans, F., Youssef, C.M., & Avolio, B.J. (2007).** *Psychological Capital: Developing the Human Competitive Edge.* Oxford University Press.
- The HERO model (Hope, Efficacy, Resilience, Optimism) — relevant for framing follow-ups in a way that protects self-efficacy rather than triggering defensiveness, especially when probing a weak answer.

---

## 7. Standards and ethics (needed if the framework will be used to assess/evaluate people)

**American Educational Research Association, American Psychological Association, & National Council on Measurement in Education (2014).** *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing.* AERA.
**Society for Industrial and Organizational Psychology (2018).** *Principles for the Validation and Use of Personnel Selection Procedures* (5th ed.).
- If probes feed into any kind of scoring/assessment, these are the governing psychometric standards for validity/fairness — reviewers in an I-O psychology or ed-tech PhD context will expect these to at least be acknowledged.

**EU AI Act (2024).** Articles on high-risk AI systems in employment contexts.
- If the framework will be used in any hiring-adjacent or automated-assessment context, this is the current regulatory reference point, particularly for anything that could function as an automated employment decision.

---

## How to use this for a client project

If your friend needs to *pick* rather than cite everything: the minimum defensible core is **Vygotsky/Wood-Bruner-Ross (where to target), Kolb (why probe at all), Ericsson (how to make it specific rather than generic), and Hattie & Timperley (how to sequence depth)**. Everything else in this list is a layer on top — tone adaptation (Higgins, Winstone), self-verification (Cable & Kay), and psychometric grounding (AERA/APA/NCME, SIOP) — that gets added depending on how sophisticated and how high-stakes the framework needs to be.
