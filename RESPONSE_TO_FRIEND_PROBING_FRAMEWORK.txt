Quick correction to what I said before I go further — I explained my probing setup assuming your framework was doing roughly the same job mine is, and it isn't. Mine is a coaching tool: the candidate practices before the real thing, and the whole system is designed to protect and develop them. Yours is going into an actual AVI (asynchronous video interview) pipeline — it's asking the real questions and evaluating real answers that feed into a real hiring decision. That's not a difference of degree, it's a difference of what the system is legally and ethically allowed to optimise for. So let me redo this properly, split into what genuinely transfers and what I'd actively warn you away from copying.

WHAT TRANSFERS — THE UNDERLYING CONSTRUCTS

1. The ZPD dual-boundary mechanism (Vygotsky, 1978) — tracking an unprompted answer separately from a post-probe answer. This actually matters MORE for you than it does for me. In coaching, the gap tells me how much support someone needs. In evaluation, that same gap tells an employer something they genuinely want to know: can this candidate produce the evidence independently, or only when led to it? That's a real, defensible signal for a hiring decision — I'd keep this, just reframe the write-up away from "developmental gap" language and toward "independent vs. prompted competency evidence," since that's the framing a validation reviewer will expect.

2. Separating structural completeness from competency verification. This one transfers directly and matters just as much, maybe more, in evaluation — a candidate following the right format without the competency being genuinely evidenced is exactly the failure mode a real hiring decision can't afford. Keep two independent fields: did they follow the expected structure, and separately, is the specific competency actually confirmed by quotable evidence. Don't collapse them into one score.

3. Tomlinson's Graduate Capital Model as a classification layer. Still relevant — employers evaluating graduates genuinely do care about more than raw skill (Human capital); Social, Cultural, Psychological and Identity capital are real predictors in the employability literature Tomlinson is drawing on. I'd keep this as an additional lens on top of verified competencies. What changes is what you do with a gap: in my coaching tool, an unevidenced capital becomes a coaching prompt ("build toward this"). In your evaluation tool, an unevidenced capital is just a finding to report — you're not there to help them fix it, you're there to accurately record that it wasn't demonstrated.

WHAT DOES NOT TRANSFER — AND WOULD ACTIVELY HURT YOUR FRAMEWORK IF IT DID

1. Adaptive personalisation. I deliberately vary scaffold level, tone, and probe difficulty per candidate — warmer framing for high-anxiety candidates, more challenge for confident ones, phase thresholds that shift based on someone's own session history. In a coaching context that's the point. In a real evaluation context, that's a fairness problem: if two candidates get different levels of scaffolding or different probe difficulty based on inferred traits, you've broken comparability across candidates, and depending on what's driving the adaptation, you may be introducing exactly the kind of inconsistency that structured-interview research (Campion, Palmer, & Campion, 1997; Campion, Pursell, & Brown, 1988) exists to eliminate. Standardization of question and probe delivery isn't a nice-to-have in a selection instrument — it's the thing that makes the psychometric properties defensible at all. If you want any adaptivity, it needs to be justified and tested for adverse impact, not just theoretically motivated.

2. The "protect self-efficacy, never criticise, frame every gap as learnable" stance (my PsyCap framing, Luthans 2007). That's a coaching posture. Your system's job is accurate measurement, not making the candidate feel good about the outcome. Warmth and protective framing in the output can actually work against validity if it's influencing what gets recorded, not just how it's phrased to the candidate.

3. My impression-management framing. I deliberately treat apparent impression management as a coaching competency, not a deception signal (Cable & Kay, 2012, self-verification) — because in my context there's no ground truth available and no real stakes if I'm wrong, so penalising it would just teach candidates to undersell themselves. You don't have that luxury. In a real AVI feeding a real decision, candidates have a genuine incentive to perform rather than reveal, and faking is a live validity threat you have to actually address, not reframe away. Levashina & Campion (2007) — the interview faking behaviour scale — is central for you in a way it's only background for me. You'll want an actual strategy here: cross-checking claims against structured evidence requirements, probe designs that make fabrication harder to sustain across follow-ups, or flagging low-specificity language as a validity concern rather than a coaching opportunity.

4. "Never compare to other candidates." That's a hard rule in my system because I never make or feed a decision. You almost certainly need the opposite — some form of norming, benchmarking, or comparability across candidates, because that's how a hiring decision gets made at all. This is worth explicitly deciding early: are you scoring against an absolute rubric (defensible independently per candidate) or relative to a candidate pool (needs a norming methodology)? Different validation requirements attach to each.

5. Treat human oversight and contestability as mandatory, not optional. I built a "flag this for review" mechanism into mine, but it's a nice-to-have — nothing downstream depends on a human reviewing it. If your framework feeds a real hiring decision, GDPR Article 22 (and the EU AI Act's high-risk classification for employment-related AI systems) means a fully automated decision without meaningful human review is a real legal exposure, not a hypothetical one. That needs to be designed in from the start — what gets reviewed, by whom, and what a candidate's route to contest a decision looks like — not bolted on later.

6. My theoretical grounding (Kolb, citing a bunch of learning-science papers) is the wrong center of gravity for you. It's fine as flavour, but the documents that actually govern whether your framework is defensible are the Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing (AERA/APA/NCME, 2014) and SIOP's Principles for the Validation and Use of Personnel Selection Procedures (5th ed., 2018). Those cover exactly what you need: reliability (test-retest, inter-rater if there's any human scoring involved), validity (content, construct, criterion — ideally validated against actual job performance or hiring outcomes, not just face validity), and adverse impact analysis across protected groups. If your PhD committee or client asks "how do you know this works," Campion et al.'s structured-interview research and these two standards documents are what you cite — not learning-cycle theory.

THE ONE-LINE VERSION

My framework asks "how do I help this specific person get better," so it's allowed to adapt, protect, and stay silent on comparison. Yours asks "how do I accurately and fairly measure this person against a standard, in a way I can defend if challenged" — which means standardize delivery, treat faking as a real threat rather than reframe it, build in human oversight from day one, and lean on psychometric validation literature (SIOP, AERA/APA/NCME, Campion et al.) as your actual foundation rather than learning-science framing. The ZPD, competency-verification split, and Tomlinson's capital model are the parts of my approach that hold up either way — everything about HOW I use them, and everything about personalisation and protective framing, needs to be rebuilt for what you're doing, not copied.
